Are Thiel's Retreats and Generals' Letters Replacing Washington?

A leaked roster and more than sixty coordinated military endorsements hint at an informal coordination network that may be supplanting formal policy channels, but the evidence for that conclusion remains unverified.

Power Theory Published November 22, 2026 · 6 sources

The Claim on the Table

Possibility, not verified It is possible that the JINSA Generals and Admirals program and Peter Thiel's Dialog retreats represent the primary structural mechanism through which U.S. foreign and economic policy is coordinated in 2026, supplanting formal institutional channels, based on the convergence of 64-plus retired flag officers signing coordinated policy letters and a leaked Dialog roster bringing together sitting governors, senators, and foreign officials at off-record gatherings [1][2]. The thesis is unverified. What follows is what the sources show, where they stop, and what they do not establish.

What the Leaks Actually Show

The Guardian reported on June 20, 2026 that a leaked registration list for Peter Thiel's Dialog retreats included 'governors, senators and congresspeople from both US political parties, leaders and officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, the United Kingdom and Japan and prominent opinion writers from outlets including the New York Times, among many others' [1]. The New American reported that 'More than six dozen retired U.S. military flag officers have signed multiple letters endorsing U.S. and Israeli aggression against Iran,' and that many of these officers are linked to the defense industry [2]. K&L Gates reported that 'Since returning to office in January 2025, President Trump has made broad assertions of executive authority, including the power to fire independent agency heads at will,' and that the Supreme Court 'has blocked the reinstatement of several other officials who are similarly contesting their at-will terminations, including from the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), Merit Systems Protection Board' [3]. These are the raw materials. None of them, standing alone, proves a coordination mechanism.

What the sources do not establish is whether the people on the Dialog list or the signatories of the JINSA letters produced specific policy outcomes, or whether their gatherings functioned as anything more than advocacy and socializing events. The Guardian's reporting documents the existence of the roster; it does not document a policy decision flowing from it [1]. The New American documents the letters and the officers' industry links; it does not trace a causal line from those letters to a change in Iran policy [2].

The 'Flex Net' Hypothesis

Possibility, not verified It is possible that the JINSA Generals and Admirals program, in which 64-plus retired flag officers coordinate policy positions on Iran through an informal network rather than through formal military or diplomatic channels, represents a concrete instance of the 'flex net' coordination Wedel describes in her 2009 book Shadow Elite, where 'flex nets' are 'close-knit networks that guard and share information' and whose members 'move seamlessly among roles in government, business, think tanks, and media,' based on the New American's reporting that these officers are 'linked to defense industry' and signed coordinated letters, and Wikipedia's description of Wedel's framework [2][4]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that the Dialog retreats, which the Guardian reported brought together sitting governors, senators, and foreign officials at off-record gatherings, function as a structural mechanism for informal policy coordination outside formal oversight, based on Wedel's own statement to the Guardian that 'It is in these sorts of gatherings – where you have financial, tech and political power coming together – that we're increasingly seeing agendas being set and opinions being shaped,' and her further claim that 'There do seem to be a growing number of fora involved in precisely this – transnational gatherings of the most powerful financial, tech and political elites, coming together. So I think it's a problem for democracy, in essence' [1].

Both possibilities rest on an analogy, not on a demonstrated causal chain. Wedel's framework, as summarized on Wikipedia, labels operators 'flexians' who advance 'their own personal agendas and those of their associates at the expense of democracy and accountability' [4]. That is a typology. Applying it to 64 retired officers who signed a letter, or to a retreat roster, requires a leap the sources do not themselves make. The evidence shows the gatherings exist and the letters were signed; it does not show that they produced specific policy outcomes or that they supplant formal institutional channels.

The Counterpoint: Formal Institutions Still Speak

The strongest case against the coordination thesis is that formal institutions—Congress, the courts, and independent agencies—remain the primary locus of governance in 2026, and that governance failures are better explained by partisan polarization or populist disruption than by elite coordination through informal networks. POGO's November 2026 analysis frames the problem as a 'constrained legislative year' requiring Congress to 'strengthen bipartisan oversight, improve accountability, and deliver results,' pointing to partisan gridlock rather than elite capture [5]. K&L Gates reports that the Supreme Court is actively redefining institutional boundaries, with the Court directed both sides to 'address whether Humphrey's Executor should be overruled' and having 'blocked the reinstatement of several other officials who are similarly contesting their at-will terminations,' indicating that formal legal channels remain the primary mechanism of institutional change [3].

Possibility, not verified It is possible that the 'outsider class' Wedel describes in her 2016 book Unaccountable is better explained by populist political dynamics—voters electing disruption over expertise—than by elite coordination through informal networks, based on the structural observation from bioengineer.org that 'Homogeneous clusters of structurally equivalent actors can create insular echo chambers, foster conformity, and induce groupthink, wherein the desire for unanimity suppresses dissenting ideas,' which describes a mechanism more consistent with populist exclusion of dissenting voices than with elite coordination, and on POGO's November 2026 analysis that Congress must 'strengthen bipartisan oversight, improve accountability, and deliver results in a constrained legislative year,' suggesting the constraint is partisan rather than elite-capture [6][5]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that the Supreme Court's active redefinition of presidential power to fire independent agency heads in 2025–2026, including the Court's direction to 'address whether Humphrey's Executor should be overruled,' represents a formal institutional mechanism that matters more than informal networks for governance outcomes, based on K&L Gates' reporting that the Court 'has blocked the reinstatement of several other officials who are similarly contesting their at-will terminations, including from the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), Merit Systems Protection Board,' indicating that formal legal channels remain the primary locus of institutional change rather than informal elite coordination [3]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that POGO's call for Congress to 'strengthen bipartisan oversight' in November 2026 reflects a formal institutional weakness that is better explained by partisan polarization and populist disruption than by Wedel's 'establishment corruption' thesis, based on the fact that POGO frames the problem as a 'constrained legislative year' requiring 'bipartisan' action, which points to partisan gridlock rather than elite capture as the primary governance failure [5].

No source in the brief makes the affirmative case that formal institutions have been fully supplanted. The sources whose evidence the coordination thesis questions—K&L Gates, POGO, and bioengineer.org—each point in the opposite direction: toward courts, Congress, and partisan dynamics as the operative forces [3][5][6].

Where the Evidence Stops

A skeptic would argue, and the brief's contradicting sources support the argument, that the Dialog leak and the JINSA letters are advocacy and socializing events, not governance mechanisms, and that the convergence of powerful figures at off-record gatherings is a necessary but not sufficient condition for policy coordination [3][5]. The evidence shows the gatherings exist; it does not show that they produce specific policy outcomes or that they supplant formal institutional channels [1][2]. What the public account establishes is a pattern of proximity—retired generals with defense-industry ties signing coordinated letters, a billionaire's retreat drawing governors and foreign officials into the same room. What it does not establish is a mechanism of governance, a causal chain from room to policy, or the displacement of courts and Congress. The question the brief poses is real. The answer, on the evidence available, is not yet in the record.
