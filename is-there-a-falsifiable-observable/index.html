Possible First Test: Can a 2026 Order Show Who Gets Locked Out of Coordination?

A November 2026 executive order may offer the first public rule for telling apart being shut out of a power network from being shut out for lack of money or rank, but no group has yet used it that way.

Bilateral Shadow Published November 23, 2026 · 4 sources

The claim

Possibility, not verified It is possible that Executive Order 14438 gives the public the first written test for telling apart being locked out of a coordination network from being locked out for lack of money or rank, a distinction that matters because it would let any excluded group check whether it is bound to answer to a node it cannot reach [1]. No source in the evidence set shows a group has used that test to act on its exclusion [2].

A List No One Was Meant to See

In June 2026, a Dialog retreats participant list surfaced in the website's source code, visible in an Internet Archive snapshot dated 15 June [3]. The list named officials from both US parties, leaders from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, the United Kingdom, and Japan, and prominent opinion writers [3]. The organization had spent two decades declining to disclose its members [3]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that this invitation-only structure, with no public membership rule, makes coordination exclusion unfalsifiable, because no excluded group can test its own exclusion without a leak [3].

In November 2026, Executive Order 14438 required federal contractors to notify the White House of congressional requests touching their work and to abide by instructions on withholding communications the White House deemed protected by executive privilege [1]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that this order supplies the first published, falsifiable criterion distinguishing coordination exclusion from resource or authority exclusion, because any contractor can check whether it is bound to coordinate with a node it cannot reach [1].

What the Iran-Contra Report Already Showed

The 1987 Iran-Contra report describes a private organization called the Enterprise that operated outside formal channels [4]. The report records that it "had its own airplanes, pilots, airfield, operatives, ship, secure communications devices, and secret Swiss bank accounts" [4]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that this shows a parallel coordination structure was historically observable in its physical infrastructure, but was never formalized as a public criterion an excluded group could use to identify its own exclusion, because the report was a one-time investigation, not a standing tool [4].

The order's text limits its coordination requirement to federal contractors and their subcontractors [1]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that this scope means workers excluded from the coordination that sets global garment wages fall outside the test, because they are not federal contractors [1].

The Case Against the Distinction

In April 2025, Lead Stories fact-checked a claim that Sen. Adam Schiff and Rep. Jamie Raskin held a "shadow government" hearing on April 7 [2]. The meeting was a "shadow hearing" — a one-party congressional meeting, distinct from a conventional committee hearing that requires both parties [2]. The term "shadow government" came from critics, not the participants [2].

Possibility, not verified It is possible that informal coordination is a normal, legitimate legislative practice, meaning exclusion from an informal node is a routine feature of institutional design, not a specific, actionable form of coordination exclusion [2]. If informal coordination is routine, then exclusion from any closed node is fully explained by authority or resource exclusion, and the "coordination" variable is redundant [2].

What the Sources Do Not Settle

No source in the evidence set shows a group identifying its own exclusion as coordination exclusion and using that label to gain entry [2]. The order's text is public, and its requirements are specific, but no excluded group has run the test and acted on the result [1][2]. The criterion is falsifiable in form: one can read the text and see who is bound. No source shows anyone has done that and changed their position [1][2].

The order binds only federal contractors and their subcontractors [1]. Workers excluded from the coordination that sets global garment wages are not federal contractors, so the test does not reach them [1]. Whether the criterion generalizes beyond that domain remains open [1].
