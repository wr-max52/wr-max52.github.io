Peer-Reviewed Necropsy Study Finds Indoor Cat Longevity Advantage Concentrated in First Year of Life

A 2022 study of 3,108 cats shows that the widely cited gap between indoor and outdoor cat lifespans is driven largely by early mortality rather than a uniform decade-long difference.

Anthropology Rehistory Published November 18, 2026 · 4 sources

What the Study Found

The largest single-institution necropsy study of cat mortality, published in PLOS One in 2022 by Kent et al., found that indoor cats live longer than outdoor cats on average, but the popular claim that indoor cats live 10 years longer than outdoor cats is overstated [1][2]. Among cats that survived past their first year, indoor, indoor-outdoor, and outdoor cats had statistically indistinguishable median lifespans of approximately 10 years, with the indoor longevity advantage concentrated in the first year of life when outdoor cats face disproportionate mortality from trauma, disease, and predation [1][2]. The finding suggests that the indoor advantage is not a uniform decade-long gap but rather a survival benefit that matters most during the earliest and most vulnerable period of a cat's life [1][2].

The Numbers Behind the Finding

The study, which examined 3,108 cases spanning 1989 to 2019, found that outdoor cats had a significantly shorter median lifespan than indoor-only cats when all ages were included, at 7.25 years compared with 9.43 years for indoor-only cats (p=0.0001) [1]. This overall difference confirms that indoor cats do live longer on average [1][2]. When the analysis was restricted to cats that survived to at least one year of age, however, the median age of death was statistically indistinguishable across housing types: 9.98 years for indoor cats, 10.09 years for indoor-outdoor cats, and 9.80 years for outdoor cats (p=0.11) [1][2]. In a multivariate regression model, environmental housing status did not have a statistically significant effect on longevity once other factors such as FeLV status and intact versus spayed or neutered status were controlled, indicating that the raw indoor-outdoor difference is partly confounded by other mortality drivers [1].

Where the Popular Figure Comes From

Popular secondary sources cite a 15-to-17-year indoor versus 2-to-5-year outdoor gap, but this figure traces to a 1990 Cat Fancy article and is not supported by the modern peer-reviewed data, which shows a much smaller and age-dependent difference [3][2]. PetMD, for instance, reports that "An indoor cat may live 15-17 years, while the life expectancy for outdoor cats is only 2-5 years, according to researchers at University of California-Davis" [3]. A separate large UK primary-care study by O'Neill et al. (2015, n=4,009) reported an overall median cat longevity of 14.0 years, with trauma the leading cause of death in cats under 5 years, consistent with outdoor exposure driving early mortality rather than a uniform lifespan penalty [4]. The UK data thus corroborates the pattern seen in the American necropsy study: outdoor cats are more likely to die young, but those that survive the early years live roughly as long as their indoor counterparts [4][1].

Counterpoint and Limitations

A Skeptics Stack Exchange analysis citing the same Kent et al. 2022 study argues that the widely repeated claim of a 10-year indoor advantage is not well supported [2]. The analysis notes that the study found no significant lifespan difference between indoor-only and indoor-outdoor cats, stating that "Indoor-outdoor cats in the sample studied do not have a significantly different lifespan from indoor cats" [2]. The analysis also points out that the dramatic gap traces to an unsourced 1990 Cat Fancy article rather than to modern data [2]. Additionally, the analysis raises the concern that the necropsy cohort excludes cats that were lost and never found, such as those killed by coyotes or cars, which could bias the outdoor group's reported lifespan upward and complicate any clean indoor-versus-outdoor comparison [2].
