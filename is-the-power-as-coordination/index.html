Is the 'Coordination Fabric' a Working Tool or a Metaphor?

A power framework promoted in 2024–2025 rests on one theorist and figurative language; no published case study yet tests it against established variables, and that gap remains unverified.

Managed Uncertainty Published November 23, 2026 · 6 sources

The claim

Possibility, not verified It is possible that Nonprofit Quarterly and Syntelos built a power framework on one scholar's metaphor rather than on tested evidence, leaving readers without a way to check whether it identifies who is excluded from decisions [1][2].

One Theory, One Author

In a 2024–2025 Syntelos article, a writer describes the moment one theory reframed how they understood power, how it flows, and how it can be changed. "Then, I found Pieter de Beer's theory, Coordination: The Fabric of Power—offering an alternative view of what power is, how it flows, and how it can be changed—and" [2]. The same theory appears in a Nonprofit Quarterly piece co-authored with de Beer [1][2]. The Syntelos article attributes the framework to one scholar, and the Nonprofit Quarterly piece reproduces his theory without citing competing coordination theories or empirical validation [1][2].

Possibility, not verified It is possible that the framework is so far a single-theorist adoption rather than a multi-source tested tool, because no competing coordination theories or empirical validation appear in either text, leaving its diagnostic claims untested against alternative explanations for exclusion [1][2].

The Language Won't Measure

The Nonprofit Quarterly article describes the fabric in metaphor: threads that "connect, they distribute weight, and they breathe" [1]. The Power Coordination substack calls imposed power "a mechanism by which some impose coordination upon others" [3]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that these figurative terms leave no test for whether a group is excluded from coordination rather than from resources, authority, or legitimacy [1][3].

EO 14438, issued in November 2026, says a coordination gap between contractors and the executive branch undermines efficiency [4]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that the order's mechanism is legal—executive privilege, notice requirements—not a distinct coordination-theoretic category [4]. The Guardian reports that Dialog retreats bring "a unique mix of government, corporate and cultural power," though "many names on the list probably attended Dialog events before Thiel's association with the political right and the Trump era" [5]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that the retreat is a long-standing convening, not a new enclosure of coordination [5]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that urban climate governance already locates exclusion in "clear nested hierarchies or concept of subsidiarity where authorities and responsibilities are neatly delineated," making coordination capacity a less specific variable [6].

The Other Side

The strongest objection is that metaphor is a teaching tool, not a flaw, and the Frontiers article concedes that "horizontal coordination has also gained significant recognition in this framework in urban climate governance scholarship" [6]. Coordination is already a recognized variable in exclusion analysis, not a novel one [6].

EO 14438 and the Dialog retreats could serve as cases where coordination capacity is the operative exclusionary mechanism [4][5]. A skeptic would argue the framework's youth shows it is untested, not untestable [6][4][5]. The test would compare its explanatory power against resource, authority, and legitimacy variables, which no source here has yet done [6][4][5].

What the Sources Don't Show

No source in this brief contains a published case study that applies the coordination framework to a specific exclusion and compares its explanatory power against resources, authority, or legitimacy [1][2][6]. The Frontiers article identifies nested hierarchies and subsidiarity as the operative exclusion variables in urban climate governance, but it does not test coordination capacity against them [6]. The Syntelos and Nonprofit Quarterly texts present the theory without empirical validation or competing theories [1][2].

The open question is whether a future case study would show coordination control explaining exclusion better than those established variables [6]. No source in this brief answers that question [1][2][3][6][4][5]. The evidence stops at metaphor and single-theorist attribution, leaving the framework's diagnostic power unproven [1][2][3].
