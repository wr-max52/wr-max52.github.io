Did Music Bond Us Before Language Did? A 135,000-Year Claim Rests on Genomics, Not Fossils

A cluster of 2025–2026 papers suggests group musicality may be an ancient social-bonding mechanism, but the evidence stops short of proving music made humans smarter or that it preceded language—a gap the sources themselves acknowledge.

Rehistory Published November 22, 2026 · 6 sources

The Thesis in Plain Terms

The public story is tidy: music bonded the tribe and paralleled language. The evidence is messier. Possibility, not verified It is possible that the cognitive infrastructure for human musicality is at least about 135,000 years old and predates the known instrument record, based on a 2026 Frontiers in Psychology article pairing population-genetic split times with cross-cultural universals [1]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that music worked as "vocal grooming," bonding groups too large for one-to-one physical grooming; a 2026 Frontiers article reports Dunbar extended his framework to music but does not test the idea [2]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that music and language both grew from a shared precursor, Steven Brown's musilanguage, neither spin-off nor source; the Wikipedia summary states the shared features are not chance parallels, but it is a structural model, not a finding confirmed by fossils or genetics [3].

What the Genomic Date Actually Says

The 2026 Frontiers article states that "pairing population genetics with cross-cultural universals situates human musicality to at least circa 135 kya, with building blocks likely extending past hominins to 6–8 mya" [1], but frames the date as a species-wide cognitive baseline, not a music-specific genotype [1]. The figure marks a cognitive possibility, not a carved flute [1]. A 2025 Nature Communications twin study, reported by ScienceDaily, estimated that 54% of variability in a Swedish sample is associated with DNA differences between individuals [4]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that a substantial share of music enjoyment is genetic rather than learned, resting on that twin-design estimate [4][5]. Yet the authors call the finding unsurprising, since "virtually every human trait is at least partly genetically influenced" [5]. A modern Swedish sample cannot reach back to the Pleistocene; it shows only that today's variation has a genetic component [5][4].

The Bonding Mechanism: Synchrony, Not Melody?

Popular accounts credit music with pumping oxytocin and building trust, but a 2026 Frontiers in Psychology scoping review complicates that picture: it reports that Riedl et al. (2017) found no effects of music on trust behavior, perceived trustworthiness, or oxytocin levels, with correlations between oxytocin and trustworthiness observed only in the no-music condition [6]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that the oxytocin-based pathway often cited for music-driven trust is weaker than popular accounts suggest, based on that null result [6].

The same review reports that Wiltermuth and Heath (2009) found synchrony was more critical than music in driving cooperation [6]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that synchrony, rather than music as such, is what drives cooperation in group-music settings, based on that finding; ritual movement or chanting without melody could produce similar bonding [6]. The review flags the question as open, resting on a single study [6].

The Counterpoint: Music as Byproduct

The strongest case against the thesis is the byproduct view: musical capacities may be spin-offs of machinery that evolved for language, hearing, and emotion, so that music's social effects need not reflect selection for music itself [1][6][5]. The genomic article treats its 135,000-year date as a species-wide cognitive baseline, not a music-specific genotype [1]; the twin-study authors call the 54% heritability unsurprising because "virtually every human trait is at least partly genetically influenced" [5]; the scoping review reports a null result on music and oxytocin [6].

Possibility, not verified It is possible that the claim that music made early humans "smarter" has no direct empirical support in the current literature, based on the fact that the cited sources address timing, bonding, trust, and enjoyment, but none measures cognitive ability or intelligence gains linked to music in early humans [1][6][5]. No source in the brief supplies the controlled studies, genetic evidence, or archaeological sequences that would show the thesis wrong [1][6][5].

What No Source Establishes

Possibility, not verified It is possible that the claim that music made early humans smarter has no direct empirical support in the current literature, based on the fact that the cited 2026 Frontiers articles, the scoping review, and the 2025 Nature Communications study address the timing of musicality, bonding, trust, and enjoyment, but none measures cognitive ability or intelligence gains linked to music in early humans [1][6][5]. The musilanguage model is a structural argument, not a finding confirmed by fossils or genetics [3]. The "vocal grooming" hypothesis is summarized in a Frontiers article that does not test it [2]. The 135,000-year figure marks a species-wide cognitive baseline, not a dated musical event [1]. What the public account presents as settled narrative, the sources present as untested possibilities, none closing the gap between a genetic date and a behavioural one [1][2][3][6][5].
