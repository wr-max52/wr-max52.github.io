Did Jazz's Credit Shift to White Musicians Through Three Quiet Mechanisms, Not One Grand Theft?

It is possible that record-company gatekeeping, a non-founder's boast, and purchased arranger charts converged between 1916 and 1936 to move jazz's public authorship toward white names—a claim the sources suggest but cannot yet prove.

Published November 22, 2026 · 11 sources

Three Mechanisms, One Shift

Possibility, not verified It is possible that the credit for jazz's invention shifted to white musicians between 1916 and 1936 not through a single act of theft but through three converging mechanisms: record companies' refusal to sign Black bands until roughly 1920, Nick LaRocca's explicit claims of sole invention beginning in the 1930s, and white bandleaders such as Paul Whiteman and Benny Goodman purchasing Black arrangers' charts while receiving public billing as the music's leaders [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]. The Smithsonian reports that "Record companies routinely ignored African-American musicians—with only a few exceptions, such as singer Bert Williams and bandleader James Reese Europe" [1], and that LaRocca "would incense many by making racist remarks and preposterously claiming that he invented jazz" [2]. Wikipedia records that detractors say Whiteman's music "co-opted the innovations of black musicians" [3], while Henderson's wife Leora said Goodman "bought Henderson's arrangements" [4].

The 'First' Recording Was a Refusal, Not a Priority

The National Park Service reports that "In 1916 the Victor Talking Machine Company offered Keppard and the Creole Orchestra an opportunity to record, but he refused" [8]. Smithsonian Magazine reports that "Record companies routinely ignored African-American musicians—with only a few exceptions, such as singer Bert Williams and bandleader James Reese Europe," and identifies the ODJB's 1917 recording as likely the first jazz recording ever issued [1]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that this designation is contingent on a single musician's refusal rather than musical priority: Keppard turned down Victor in 1916, and Mamie Smith's 1920 "Crazy Blues" sold 75,000 copies to a majority-black audience in its first month, showing a Black commercial market existed but had not yet been courted by major labels [1][8][5].

The Boaster and the Buyers

Wikipedia records that in 1916 LaRocca "was chosen as a last-minute replacement for Frank Christian in Johnny Stein's band to play a job up in Chicago, Illinois" [6]. Smithsonian Magazine reports that "in later years, leader LaRocca would incense many by making racist remarks and preposterously claiming that he invented jazz" [2]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that LaRocca's repeated public claims of sole invention in the 1930s through the 1950s did more to entrench a white-origin narrative than any single recording, because he was not a founder of the New Orleans jazz tradition but a last-minute replacement, meaning the white-invention story was sustained by a non-founder's self-promotion rather than by a documented historical sequence [2][6].

Wikipedia's Fletcher Henderson article reports that Henderson's wife Leora said "John Hammond and Goodman bought Henderson's arrangements to support him, that Goodman always gave Henderson credit for the arrangements and said that he played them better than his own" [4]. Wikipedia also notes that detractors suggest Whiteman's music "co-opted the innovations of black musicians" [3], and that Whiteman "was classically trained but liked jazz, associating it closely with the Original Dixieland Jazz Band and other white purveyors of the music" [7]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that Paul Whiteman and Benny Goodman received public billing as jazz's leaders while their musical content was substantially authored by Black arrangers, based on these reports, leaving open whether Goodman's credit practice was typical or an exception, since no contemporary billing or royalty records were found in these sources [3][4][7].

The Structural Break and the Packaging Event

Dave Lippman's essay reports that "French Creole musicians downtown had been playing in marching and dance bands; but in 1894, creoles were expelled from polite White society under new segregation laws" [9]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that this expulsion created a structural break making Black and Creole origins less visible to later white audiences, though Lippman's piece is an advocacy essay and no primary legal record in the sources consulted corroborates the claim [9].

The National Park Service describes the ODJB as an "instant hit" that drew interest from Victor and Columbia, "who were eager to exploit the new 'jazz craze'" [10]. Possibility, not verified It is possible that the NPS's framing—treating the white band's breakthrough as the culmination of a musical revolution begun in the 1890s by Buddy Bolden—implicitly concedes that the event was packaging rather than invention, and that the "jazz craze" was a market phenomenon attaching white names to a tradition already in circulation [10].

The Case Against Erasure

The same Smithsonian article that documents label gatekeeping also reports that "in New Orleans, where there was little racial segregation, blacks and whites lived cheek-by-jowl, everyone's windows were open and sounds floated from house to house, which meant music was easily shared" [11]. If the music was genuinely co-created across racial lines in a low-segregation environment, the shift of credit to white bands may reflect commercial packaging and individual boasting rather than a structural erasure of Black authorship [11]. No source in the brief documents a coordinated effort to suppress Black names; the evidence assembled here shows uneven visibility, not a single act of theft [1][2][10][3][4][7]. A skeptic would argue that white musicians were inside the tradition, not outside it, and that the gatekeeping described by the Smithsonian and the National Park Service is a market failure rather than a conspiracy [1][10].
